Teshuva

Reuven Shina Talmid Chacham

Question

Why does וְהַחֲזִירֵנוּ בִּתְשׁוּבָה come after the request for atonement/forgiveness/pardon, not before?

This is from the יְהִי רָצוֹן recited after Tehillim:

שֶׁתְכַפֶּר לָנוּ עַל כָּל חַטֹאתֵינוּ וְתִסְלָח לָנוּ עַל כָּל עֲווֹנוֹתֵינוּ, וְתִמְחַל לָנוּ עַל כָּל פְּשָׁעֵינוּ שֶׁחָטָאנוּ וְשֶעָוִינוּ וְשֶׁפָּשַעְנוּ לְפָנֶיךָ. וְהַחֲזִירֵנוּ בִּתְשׁוּבָה שְׁלֵמָה לְפָנֶיךָ...

We first ask Hashem to grant us kappara (atonement), selicha (forgiveness), and mechila (pardon) for our chataim, avonot, and pesha'im — and only afterward do we ask Him to bring us back in complete teshuva.

This seems backwards. Per the Rambam( Hilchot Teshuva 1/1) teshuva is a prerequisite for kappara — one must first repent, and only then can forgiveness follow. Why, then, does this tefillah ask for forgiveness first and teshuva second? Shouldn't the request for "וְהַחֲזִירֵנוּ בִּתְשׁוּבָה" precede the requests for כפרה/סליחה/מחילה rather than follow them?

Answer

The safest reading is that the Yehi Ratzon is not laying out a strict timeline; it is collecting several related requests: first that Hashem help us return, and also that He forgive and atone for what we already did. In other words, the order reflects liturgical style, not a claim that forgiveness literally comes before repentance. [1]

Source 3 makes the same move: it asks, “return us in complete repentance” and then continues, “stretch out Your hand... to return us in complete repentance” and “guide us to Your service.”. That is, the prayer asks for the inner return itself as something God enables, not merely something that must be completed before any further request can be said. So the structure can be read as: help us return, and in that same mercy accept us. [3]

Source 2 is especially close to your question because it is another repentance-related prayer that explicitly places the request to be received “in teshuva” alongside requests “to forgive, pardon, and atone” for sins. That source shows that liturgy can speak of divine acceptance into teshuva and divine forgiveness in one sequence without intending a rigid order of causes and effects. [2]

The Rambam point you raised is still relevant, but only to the halachic-theological framework, not necessarily to the wording order. [5]

Source 1 also helps correct the premise that teshuva must always be treated as a human step completed entirely before any divine response. There, the petitioner asks God to “bring us back in complete repentance,” and the commentary says we ask daily for divine guidance in repenting because God “leads” the sinner back. That supports reading the prayer as asking for God’s assistance in repentance itself, not as a self-contained human prerequisite placed before all other requests. [1]

So the best supported answer is: the Yehi Ratzon is not backwards; it is devotional language that bundles together the return itself and the mercy that follows it. The prayer is asking, in one breath, for God to bring us back, forgive us, and complete what we cannot complete on our own. What is not established by the sources is a precise grammatical or historical explanation of why this exact phrasing was arranged in this order. [2]

Still open

  • A source that explicitly explains the exact ordering in this specific Yehi Ratzon after Tehillim, rather than only similar repentance prayers or the general theology of teshuva and kapparah.
  • The sources support a general explanation from similar prayers and teshuva/kapparah theology, but none explicitly explain the exact ordering of this specific Yehi Ratzon after Tehillim.

[4] What are the differences between selichah, mechilah and kaparah?

miyodeya

Answer 1 (Shmuel Goldstein): To better understand the core meaning of the word כַּפֶּר, it would be beneficial to compare and contrast it with similar Hebrew words. As part of a familiar phrase found in the Machzor of Yom Kippur, we have three such words all grouped together. This is the phrase of סְלַח לָֽנוּ - Pardon us, מְחַל לָֽנוּ - Forgive us, כַּפֶּר לָֽנוּ - Absolve us. As is the case with Hebrew words, especially those found within Torah, each of these has its own distinct meaning. Since the word מְחַל doesn’t appear once in TaNaKh in the context of forgiveness, we’ll first focus on the words סְלַח and כַּפֶּר. Rabbeinu Bachya writes, “There is a difference between what is described as כַּפָּרָה“atonement,” and סְלִיחָה, “forgiveness.” The word סְלִיחָה means that the guilt has been forgiven לְגַמְרֵי - totally, is now “non-existent” whereas the former is merely concerned with culpability for the sin committed.” [Rabbeinu Bachya ben Asher- Vayikra 5:10.] Also, the notes in the Artscroll Machzor of Yom Kippur explain, “According to Malbim [See also Vayikra 4:26 where the Malbim explains that כַּפָּרָה is what the sinner needs to do and סְלִיחָה is what we ask of HaShem.] and R' Hirsch, these three terms go from a greater degree of forgiveness to a smaller degree. סְלַח is the idea of total forgiveness; מְחַל means a permanent deferral of punishment, even though the sin has not been completely forgiven; כַּפֶּר - from כפרה, cover - implies that the sin is hidden from sight so that the punishment is suspended temporarily.” [Artscroll’s Yom Kippur Machzor, page 860. C.f. Shenei Luchos HaBris, Aseres HaDibros, Yoma, Ner Mitzva 1, where he states that these three words indicate an increasing level of forgiveness corresponding to decreasing levels of sinfulness.] Elsewhere, Rav Sampson Rafael Hirsch explains this root word כַּפֶּר on its first appearance when describing the instructions for the building of the Ark for Noach. It says, “וְכָפַרְתָּ אֹתָהּ מִבַּיִת וּמִחוּץ בַּכֹּפֶר… - and you must cover it both inside and outside with pitch.” HaRav Hirsch explains, [The Hirsch Chumash, Bereishis 6:14, pg. 182.] “The basic meaning of כפר (related to: גבר - strength, קבר - bury) is a protective or restrictive covering. It protects something so that nothing from the outside affects it. So is the case here, and the same is it elsewhere on the word כַּפֹּרֶת.” [Shemos 25:17.] As mentioned above, the word מְחַל doesn’t appear once in TaNaKh in the context of forgiveness but also has the meaning of a type of covering, a cave, which offers protection from harm. In Yeshayahu it says, “וּבִמְחִלּוֹת עָפָר - and into the hollows of the earth…” where Rashi explains: מחלות: הם הם מערות, i.e., caves. [Yeshayahu 2:19.] Avraham’s nephew, Lot, also sought out protection in a cave while Sodom and its sister cities were being destroyed.[Midrash HaGadol, Bereishis 19:30.] Dovid HaMelech also sought out refuge in the same cave of Adullam[Sefer HaYashar ad.loc.] with his family while escaping from King Shaul. [I Samuel 21:11-16, 22:1.] So it can be suggested that the phrase “סְלַח לָֽנוּ - Pardon us, מְחַל לָֽנוּ - Forgive us, כַּפֶּר לָֽנוּ - Absolve us” can be understood as follows; “סְלַח לָֽנוּ - Pardon us completely. And if not, at least מְחַל לָֽנוּ - Forgive us, and protect us as if in a cave so we may not suffer punishment. And if not, at least כַּפֶּר לָֽנוּ - Forgive us – cover and shield us at least temporarily to avoid punishment.[ C.f. Ibn Ezra on אֲכַפְּרָה פָנָיו, Bereishis 32:21.] {From the upcoming sefer Divrei Shaagasi } Answer 2 (Dov): In the back of the Artscroll Machzor on Yom Kippur (p.860) on the part of vidui where we read: ועל כֻּלָּם אֱלֽוֹהַּ סְלִיחוֹת. סְלַח לָֽנוּ. מְחַל לָֽנוּ. כַּפֶּר לָֽנוּ And for all of these, G-d of pardon, pardon us, forgive us, grant us atonement. It writes as follows: This is a progression from a smaller to a greater degree of forgiveness. סלח, forgive us , i.e. give up the right to punish us, מחל, pardon us , i.e. do not even harbour resentment or ill will against us; and כפר, atone for us , i.e. remove any effects of our sin as if we had never committed them. ( Abarbanel ) According to Malbim and R' Hirsch , these terms go from a greater degree of forgiveness to a smaller degree. סלח connotes total forgiveness; מחל means a permanent waiver of the right to punish, even though the sin has not been completely forgiven; כפר - from כפרה, cove r - implies only that the sin is hidden from sight, so that the punishment is suspended temporarily. In Pathway to Prayer by Rabbi Mayer Birnbaum he adopts the first approach, and translates it as: סְלַח לָֽנוּ - Forgive us for the sins themselves מְחַל לָֽנוּ - Pardon us for the insult caused to Your Honor by our sins כַּפֶּר לָֽנוּ - (and) atone for us as if they had never occurred. Answer 3 (shmosel): The Shaloh explains that they're increasing level of forgiveness corresponding to decreasing levels of sinfulness: כפרה לחטאים. ומחילה לעונות. וסליחה לפשעים שחטא הוא השוגג מתפללים שימחקם לגמרי כאלו לא היו מעולם. וזה ל' כפרה כמו אכפרה פניו וגו'. ומחילה לעון כי שם נשאר שום רושם. וסליחה שהיא אריכות אף לפושעים הם המורדים כי עליהם יום הכפורים תולה For חטא, which is an inadvertent sin, we pray that He should erase them completely, like they never happened. This is [indicated by] the term כפרה, similar to "I will appease him." (Genesis 32:21) [The weaker term] מחילה corresponds to עון, because it leaves some trace behind. סליחה connotes clemency to פושעים, the wanton sinners, because Yom Kippur only suspends [judgement] for them.

[6] Does Teshuva protect from punishment or not?

miyodeya · Benyomin Walters

Perhaps an answer: There is punishment for the sin, which is for the rebellion against G-d. This is immediately shielded by repentance. Then, there is the process of complete atonement, which is more of a refinement or educational processes. This may require suffering to complete. Yet, this suffering is not a punishment. For example, when the Jewish people repeatedly sinned in the desert, G-d immediately forgives them each time. Yet, after the sin of the spies, it is decreed that they will spend 40 years in the desert--despite the fact that they were already forgiven. The 40 years in the desert was not a punishment for their rebellion, but a process of refinement so that they would have the necessary faith to enter the land of Israel. (This answer is based on a talk by the Lubavitcher Rebbe, but I don't have the exact reference now. I'll try to add it later.)

Answer 1 (rikitikitembo): Rambam Hilchot Teshuva 1:4 (sefaria translation) עָבַר עַל כְּרֵתוֹת וּמִיתוֹת בֵּית דִּין וְעָשָׂה תְּשׁוּבָה, תְּשׁוּבָה וְיוֹם הַכִּפּוּרִים תּוֹלִין וְיִסּוּרִין הַבָּאִין עָלָיו גּוֹמְרִין לוֹ הַכַּפָּרָה. וּלְעוֹלָם אֵין מִתְכַּפֵּר לוֹ כַּפָּרָה גְּמוּרָה עַד שֶׁיָּבוֹאוּ עָלָיו יִסּוּרִין, וּבְאֵלּוּ נֶאֱמַר (תהילים פט לג) "וּפָקַדְתִּי בְשֵׁבֶט פִּשְׁעָם וּבִנְגָעִים עֲוֹנָם" If a person violates [sins punishable by] karet or execution by the court and repents, Teshuvah and Yom Kippur have a tentative effect and the sufferings which come upon him complete the atonement. He will never achieve complete atonement until he endures suffering for concerning these [sins, Psalms 89:33] states: "I will punish their transgression with a rod." This is based on Yoma 86a - עָבַר עַל כָּרֵיתוֹת וּמִיתוֹת בֵּית דִּין וְעָשָׂה תְּשׁוּבָה — תְּשׁוּבָה וְיוֹם הַכִּפּוּרִים תּוֹלִין, וְיִסּוּרִין מְמָרְקִין. Answer 2 (Chatzkel): Berachos 5a וְהַיְינוּ דְּרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן לָקִישׁ, דְּאָמַר רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן לָקִישׁ: נֶאֱמַר ״בְּרִית״ בְּמֶלַח, וְנֶאֱמַר ״בְּרִית״ בְּיִסּוּרִין, נֶאֱמַר ״בְּרִית״ בְּמֶלַח, דִּכְתִיב: ״וְלֹא תַשְׁבִּית מֶלַח בְּרִית״ וְנֶאֱמַר ״בְּרִית״ בְּיִסּוּרִין, דִּכְתִיב: ״אֵלֶּה דִבְרֵי הַבְּרִית״, מַה ״בְּרִית״ הָאָמוּר בְּמֶלַח — מֶלַח מְמַתֶּקֶת אֶת הַבָּשָׂר, אַף ״בְּרִית״ הָאָמוּר בְּיִסּוּרִין — יִסּוּרִין מְמָרְקִין כׇּל עֲוֹנוֹתָיו שֶׁל אָדָם . And that is the statement of Rabbi Shimon ben Lakish, as Rabbi Shimon ben Lakish said: The word covenant is used with regard to salt, and the word covenant is used with regard to afflictions. The word covenant is used with regard to salt, as it is written: “The salt of the covenant with your God should not be excluded from your meal-offering; with all your sacrifices you must offer salt” (Leviticus 2:13). And the word covenant is used with regard to afflictions, as it is written: “These are the words of the covenant” (Deuteronomy 28:69). Just as, in the covenant mentioned with regard to salt, the salt sweetens the taste of the meat and renders it edible, so too in the covenant mentioned with regard to suffering, the suffering cleanses a person’s transgressions, purifying him for a more sublime existence. Answer 3 (mbloch): The Ramchal discusses this in Derech Hashem, part 2, chapter 2, where he writes that suffering allows to purify a mostly righteous person and get him ready for the World to Come God gave suffering the power to dispel the insensitivity in man, allowing him to become pure and clear , prepared for the ultimate good at its appointed time. The amount of suffering ( issurim ) needed to purify the individual would then depend on the amount of insensitivity that he has acquired as a result of his deeds. (translation R Aryeh Kaplan, p. 101) Similarly in Daat Tevunot, ch. 3, part 4 (p. 121 in the Feldheim edition) God only wants His creations to be perfected and does not completely reject the wicked; on the contrary, He purifies them through the fire of suffering so that they will emerge cleansed of all their dross . The Rambam as well mentions (MT Hilchot Tshuva 1:4), based on the gemara in Yoma 86a , that repentance and suffering give atonement for certain classes of sins If a person violates [sins punishable by] karet or execution by the court and repents, Teshuvah and Yom Kippur have a tentative effect and the sufferings which come upon him complete the atonement . He will never achieve complete atonement until he endures suffering for concerning these [sins, Psalms 89:33] states: "I will punish their transgression with a rod."

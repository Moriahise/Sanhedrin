Question

Memallel vs. M'daber vs. M'Siach

Related to this question and the comments on it, because originally by mistake I called the speaking kingdom Medaber instead of Memallel.

Are porpoises considered Memallel or Chai? https://judaism.stackexchange.com/questions/157236

Why is Memallel chosen to be the label for the fourth kingdom? I take it part of it is that speech that is not heard or accepted as per below, is not called Dibbur, so I can see what Medaber is. Sicha is casual conversation. Mila refers to words.

https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Translation:Likutei_Etzot#DIBBUR_-_Speech

Speech (Dibbur) that doesn't get heard or accepted can't be called "speech" at all. Tikkun haDibbur, when it's heard and accepted, comes by saying words of praise of the True Tzaddikim. This is Tikkun haKlali, General Remedy, for your speech (29:1,2).
But what is Memallel versus now there's Dinbur and Mila so two things to differentiate from, and the answer coming to me, that Memallel is one who utters words. Medaber is someone whose speech (or written words?) is heard or accepted. M'Siach is someone who converses, that you can chat with. Is this correct, or no?

Also, Dibbur can refer to written speech, so one could be illiterate yet still speaks the words of the language and is therefore Memallel. I guess M'daber is therefore more inclusive, because it includes also up someone who writes but doesn't speak. Since speech came and comes before writing, I guess I've answered my own question. Unless I'm mistaken

Answer

A porpoise cannot be called a "memallel" in the same sense as a human being. The texts place "memallel / medaber" on the human side of the human-animal divide. Animals are simply "chai". [2] [1]

The strongest explicit source for the terminology is the Onkelos translation of Genesis 2:7. Which says האדם became "ruach memallla"—a speaking spirit. [1]

Rashi’s gloss makes the distinction sharper. He says that man was given extra "daat and dibbur,". So on these sources, the boundary is not merely "can emit sounds". [2]

The broader fourfold categorization in the supplied material also places animals under "chai" and humans under "medaber.". One source summarizes this as beings with speech, whether oral or written, being the medaber category. So if one is asking for a creation-category label, a porpoise would remain in the animal class rather than moving into the speaking class. [4] [10]

On "m'siach," the sources support a different nuance: "sicha" is conversation, especially light or idle talk, while "dibbur" is the more general term for speech and can include serious or practical matters. Yein Levanon and Midrash Shmuel both say that "sicha" often means words of little substance or unnecessary chatter. While practical household speech is still called "dibbur.". [5] [6]

So the hypothesis in your question needs a correction. The sources do not support treating "memallel" as mere utterance, "m'daber" as heard/accepted speech, and "m'siach" as chat. The special idea that unheard speech is not real speech appears only in a devotional source about tikkun ha-dibbur, not as a general dictionary definition. [8] [5]

Likewise, the spiritual language in Likutei Etzot should not be turned into a universal lexical rule. That source speaks about repairing one's speech in a religious sense, not about defining Hebrew vocabulary across all usages. So it may be relevant to a teaching about speech's value, but it does not by itself define memallel, medaber, or sicha as technical categories. [8]

Based on the supplied sources, a porpoise is best left in the "chai" category. While "memallel / medaber" belongs to humans as the speaking creature;. "sicha" is casual or idle conversation, a narrower use of speech. What cannot be proven from these sources is any special porpoise classification or a strict technical split between "memallel" and "m'daber" beyond the ordinary human-speech usage. [4] [1] [5] [8]

Still open

  • The draft does not establish a technical lexical split between “memallel” and “m’daber”; the sources support human speech language generally, not a separate rule for porpoises.

[4] What does midbar mean?

miyodeya

Answer 1 (Yaacov Deane): The noun Midbar is best translated as wilderness , not necessarily desert . The distinction being that a wilderness is a place devoid of human habitation and settlements. The term desert , although coincidentally usually not conducive to human settlement, is only about a lack of precipitation, rainfall or snowfall. And to emphasize, it doesn’t necessarily imply a lack of availability of water. A desert can be filled with wellsprings, underground aquifers or like the continent of Antarctica can be covered with ice and snow because whatever precipitation has ever fallen there over the millennia has not evaporated because of the extreme and continuous cold. So returning to the subject of wilderness being devoid of human settlement, the Torah, in its categorization of different types of creations, has four general categories of: inanimate objects דומם (things which are silent and do not have their own power of locomotion), plants צומח (things which sprout, usually, but not limited to sprouting from the ground), animals חיות (meaning things which do not sprout and which possess the power of self-locomotion. They may also be able to make noises and even possess some form of communication other than speech.) and humanoids מדבר (beings which have the power of speech, either oral or written or both). And this underlines the association of wilderness מדבר (pronounced Midbar) and humanoid מדבר (pronounced M’Daber, meaning speak or speech) settlement. That a wilderness can be profusely filled with all kinds of life, but is devoid of speech. Answer 2 (Shmuel): Rabbi Yonathan Sacks z"l explained once that the meaning behind the word "midbar" is to mean (COVENANT & CONVERSATION The Wilderness and the Word, Bamidbar 5768) : The Hebrew word midbar, wilderness, has the same root as the word dabar/davar, meaning “word” or “thing.” It has the same letters as medabber, “speaking.” It is in the wilderness that the Israelites hear revelation, the word or speaking of G-d There's a famous Midrash (see for example here ) that explains why the Torah has been given in the midbar. Why was the Torah not given in the Holy Land, Eretz Yisrael? The answer lies, tells the Midrash, in the fact that a midbar has no ownership on itself. A midbar belongs to no one. So too, everyone has an equal claim to the Torah (see also Midrash Tanchuma, Chukas 21:1) Elsewhere , Rabbi Sacks explains the deeper meaning behind the reason why the Torah was given in the midbar: The giving of the Torah in the wilderness is an essential feature of Jewish history. Had the Israelites received the Torah in the land, it would be indissolubly associated with the land. Exile would mean the end of the covenant. It would make no more sense to keep Torah while in exile than to obey the laws of Russia while living in Spain. What made the G-d of Israel different was the fact that He was sovereign of the universe, not a local deity. That is why the Jewish people survived dispersion. Only the G-d of everywhere can be found and worshipped anywhere. I would strongly recommend this article, to further grasp the essence of the word "midbar" and the relationship between the giving of the Torah and us being in exile. https://www.rabbisacks.org/covenant-conversation/bamidbar/wilderness-and-revelation/

[15] Why does plant life come after the earth, waters and the firmament, but before the planets?

miyodeya

Answer 1 (Al Berko): The reason seems to be explained by Rambam in Yesodey Hatorah 3.9 : "All of the stars and spheres are beings, endowed with a soul , intelligence and understanding; they continue a purposeful life and are conscious of the existence of Him Who spoke and the universe sprang forth." With this logic, plants were not created with soul, just as the earth, the waters, and the firmament, they lack psyche (see Aristotle's view ). Therefore they can't move (intentionally), they can only grow. Therefore they are created after the basic elements of Earth and water that share this feature. But the celestial bodies do move, and therefore they possess a (basic form of) Nefesh (psyche) that "drives" them. Of course, their movement is limited by their perfectness, so they only move steadily in circles. And of course, then come animals that move intentionally and sporadically. In other words, in the philosophical division of objects into דומם, צומח וחי (inanimate, plant, living), the planets fit into the third category, and therefore follow the plants in creation. Answer 2 (Micha Berger): The order of creation is in two parallel sets of three. (This is often attributed to the Maharal, but I think the idea doesn't emerge until the 20th cent, and then in a number of sources.) On day one Hashem created light and separates light from darkness, creating the notion of day. On day four, the first day of the second set, He creates "lights in the raqia (?) of the heaven to divide the day from the night". (Rishonim offer too many possible translations of raqia for me to pick sides.) One day two -- separates the waters above the raqia from those below. And on the corresponding day in the second set, day 5, He populates them with fish and birds. On the last day of the first triad (day 3), Hashem made land to stand on. And on the last day of the second triad (day 6) -- the animals and people who live on it. This would imply that the sequence of creation only makes sense in metaphysical / spiritual terms, not in physical and causal ones.

Protection of schach from getting moldy

This year we have much rain in the NY-NJ area during Succot. This may cause danger going forward that bamboo mats used for schach would get moldy.

What can one recommend for protecting the schach from getting moldy? If the schach gets moldy, how can one clean it and kill the mold?

Bamboo mats are commonly used for schach due to their properties: they grow from the ground, are currently detached, and are not susceptible to ritual impurity, making them kosher for schach. Bamboo is also sturdy and resists deterioration from sun and rain during Succot, which often lasts only a week. Bamboo mats, if certified and manufactured specifically for schach, are valid and do not become tamei. However, they should not be connected permanently with nails or metal fasteners, as this may invalidate the schach due to the prohibition of 'taaseh velo min ha'asui'. Alternatives include resting the bamboo mats on supports or temporarily fastening them for the holiday only. The width of individual bamboo pieces is also a factor, with thinner pieces less problematic. [4] [5] [11]

Regarding protection against mold and moisture, no sources directly discuss mold prevention on bamboo mats used as schach. However, general preservation practice for similar items such as Torah scrolls suggests airing is essential to prevent musty odors and mold growth. A sofer recommends periodically unrolling and airing scrolls to allow each part to be exposed to air, avoiding moisture buildup. Covers made from thick or synthetic materials reduce breathability and could contribute to mold. Changing and washing covers regularly is advised. Applying these insights, it may be beneficial to air the schach mats during Succot or before storage, avoid covering with impermeable materials, and keep them dry. [3]

If the schach becomes moldy, physical removal of mold by wiping with a natural, effective cleaner is recommended. Plain white vinegar has been found to be effective against mold. After cleaning, thorough drying and ventilation are critical to prevent recurrence. Mechanical removal, wiping down with vinegar solution, and airing the mats in a well-ventilated area are practical steps, bearing in mind that chemical sprays or bleach are not advisable due to possible damage or health concerns. Ozone treatment is noted for mold odor remediation in other contexts but might pose risks for delicate materials and would involve special equipment and precautions. Moisture absorbers such as silica gel packets could reduce humidity in storage areas if practical. [3]

To secure schach mats against wind without invalidating the sukkah, it is preferable to use supports made from materials not susceptible to ritual impurity. For lightweight bamboo mats vulnerable to wind, placing heavier objects like wooden planks (e.g., 2x4s) over the mats or tying them with natural materials such as reed twine or palm fiber is permitted. Ropes or twine used to hold the schach should ideally not be made from spun or woven fabrics which receive ritual impurity. Using monofilament fishing line, which is not mekabel tumah, is allowed for securing bamboo slats. Nails or permanent fastenings are prohibited as they resemble permanent structures. Looping the ends of bamboo mats around wooden slats is a practical method to secure schach from being blown off. [7] [9] [10] [12] [8]

Summary: To mitigate mold risk on bamboo schach during rainy Succot seasons, one should keep the mats as dry as possible, allow air circulation by airing them frequently, avoid impermeable covers, and remove mold promptly by physical cleaning with natural agents like white vinegar, followed by thorough drying. For securing schach, avoid permanent fixtures and use permissible natural ties or weight the mats properly. Certified bamboo schach not held together by prohibited materials is kosher, and care should be taken not to invalidate schach by improper attachment. [3] [4] [7] [9] [10] [11] [12]

[6] What Is Sechach?

chabad · Yehuda Shurpin

According to Jewish law, there are a few main requirements for the covering of the sukkah (called sechach): the material must have grown from the ground; it must be presently detached from the ground; and it should not be susceptible to ritual impurity (which in layman’s terms means that it should be unprocessed, not fashioned into a tool or something useful and not anything edible such as fruits or produce)1. Cheap Remnants The Talmud2 derives these requirements from the verse “You shall prepare for you the festival of Sukkot for seven days as you gather from your threshing floor and from your winepress.”3 This, the rabbis explain, implies that the holiday is to be celebrated with materials that are typically left on a threshing floor or in a winepress, such as straw, stalks, and reeds. The defining element of the sukkah is the sechach covering, so this verse is essentially saying that the sechach should be made of natural, unprocessed materials that grow from the ground and are not susceptible to ritual impurity. What Is Commonly Used for Sechach? There is room for creativity here, since so many types fit the bill, as long as it grew from the ground, has been harvested and not been made into anything. When selecting your sechach type, make sure it is not something that will shrivel up during the course of sukkot, as many leafy greens would. As such, the following items have become favorites: Palm Fronds: This is especially popular in Israel and in warmer climates. Evergreen Branches: This is common in the northeastern US Corn Stalks: These are increasingly popular in the Midwest, where they are in season during the fall. Bamboo: This can be saved from year to year. In recent decades, many have begun using rabbinically-certified bamboo mats, which are convenient. Many people may even choose a mix, making a “bed” of bamboo, which they then cover with evergreen. But what is the deeper significance of this mitzvah? Real Love Is in Simplicity Throughout the month of Elul and the High Holidays, we work constantly on improving ourselves and returning to G‑d. The holiday of Sukkot celebrates the completion of this work and the assurance of a year full of closeness to the Divine. This is one of the reasons why we refer to the holiday of Sukkot as “the time of our rejoicing.” The Chassidic masters explain that in addition to representing the miraculous Clouds of Glory that surrounded the Jews in the desert for forty years, the covering of the sukkah symbolizes the culmination of our refinement process during the month of Elul and the High Holidays. The climax of the High Holiday services was the Kohen Gadol (High Priest) entering the Holy of Holies and offering ketoret (incense), which would form a thick cloud, representing G‑d’s glory and the culmination of our personal refinement. Rabbi Dovber of Lubavitch teaches that the correlation between clouds and the process of refinement can be found in the verse “And a mist ascended from the earth and watered the entire surface of the ground.”4 Mist ascending from the ground represents the uplifting and refining of our earthly actions, until they rise up to heaven and form “clouds,” from which “rain” (blessings) pours down upon us.5 Interestingly, the Talmud correlates this same verse with the sechach covering of the sukkah (since the sechach represents the clouds), going so far as to derive some of the laws pertaining to the sechach from this verse.6 The mystics explain that while the Yom Kippur services represent the culmination of our refining ourselves, the sukkah, and specifically the sechach covering, corresponds to the Clouds of Glory and the cloud of the ketoret, symbolizing how G‑d reciprocates and showers us with his blessings and love.7 So when G‑d instructs us to build a sukkah, He tells us that the covering should not be made of expensive materials, like gold and silver, nor even any edible items. Rather, it should be made from reeds and other “refuse.” For G‑d is saying, “Even if you seem to have no spiritual advantage or superiority, no gold or silver, no love or awe of Me, just simple, leftover reeds, I still love you and bless you.”8 Primordial Purity Rabbi Dovber explains elsewhere that the sechach covering of the sukkah represents an all-encompassing revelation of G‑dliness that is completely pure, reminiscent of the revelation that was present in the world before the sin of Adam and Eve, as well as the revelation that will be present in the Messianic era. The sechach therefore needs to be made from material that grows from the ground and isn’t susceptible to impurity, representing the Earth in its pure, pre-sin state.9 May the day come soon when, in the words of the prophet Zechariah,10 all the nations of the world will go up to the Holy Temple to celebrate the holiday of Sukkot! So Star-K brings a good breakdown based on a psak of Rav Moshe Heineman: In Hilchos Sukkah ( Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim 629:1 ) it is written that schach can not consist of something that is “mekabel tumah” (something that has the ability to become ritually impure). It is for this reason that bamboo carpet mats cannot be used for schach as they are mekabel tumah. All Star-K certified schach is not made to sit or walk on and is therefore not mekabel tumah. In addition, l’chatchila, kosher schach may not be supported by anything which is mekabel tumah (see Rama O.C. 629:7 ). According to the opinion of Rashi (as brought in Shaar Hatziyun 629:20 ) spun or woven threads (e.g. string, yarn) are mekabel tumah. Rav Moshe Feinstein states ( Igros Moshe, O.C. 1:177 ) based on a Mishna, if something which is mekabel tumah is used to hold wooden slats together, the slats themselves are also mekabel tumah and are no longer kosher for schach use. Therefore, Rav Moshe Feinstein explains that wooden venetian blinds held together with cloth tape or string are not kosher for schach. It follows that according to Rashi bamboo slats held together by multi-filament cord (i.e. it is braided or twisted) are also not kosher for schach (even if they are not made to sit or walk on). However, monofilament (commonly used for fishing line) is not woven or spun material. Therefore, it is not mekabel tumah and may be used to hold bamboo slats together. All Star-K certified bamboo schach is held together with monofilament cord. So based on this, it would only be an issue if the string is woven or spun which would make it mekabel tumah. If it isn't it would be allowed to hold the slats together.

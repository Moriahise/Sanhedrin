Question

Piyutim in Pre-Expulsion England

A brief scan of the Goldschmidt-Fränkel machazorim will readily yield the conclusion that all mediæval Ashkenazi communities had a tradition of reciting piyutim, although as far as I'm aware, only the German https://www.kajinc.org/about/nusach and Polish https://ggbh.info/ customs remain.

In addition to these currently-extant corpuses, the above-mentioned machazorim print variant selections, according to the French, Mainz, and other continental Ashkenazi nuschaot. Glaringly absent is the piyut liturgy of Nusach Anglia, the pre-Expulsion English nusach.*

Given that all other Ashkenazi communities maintained orders of piyutim to be inserted on [usually special] Shabbatot and holidays, this would also have been the case in England. Do we have any extant attestation to which piyutim were part of the mediæval Nusach Anglia and when they were said ?

*Not to be confused with Nusach Anglia, the modern English Rite, itself a descendant of the more conservative Western European recension of Nusach Polen. The mediæval Nusach Anglia was related to Nusach Tzorfas, the extinct French nusach, which is sufficiently similar to Nusach Ashkenaz, although some differences did exist.

Answer

Machzor Vitry. Described here as reflecting the customs of Jews in early medieval France. And as a basis for Nusach Ashkenaz. [1]

That confirms that the Ashkenazic tradition had systematic sets of occasional piyutim. [2]

The cited discussion says that in western Ashkenaz it was never said. While in French usage it was said in every Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur kerovah. [4]

The same source also indicates that piyutim had different insertion points within the prayers. Including morning, Musaf, and the Yotzer/kerovah framework. And that Ashkenazic communities differed in practice. [3]

From these sources alone, English medieval piyut practice is only indirectly reconstructible from related French and general Ashkenazic evidence. You cannot yet list which poems were definitely used in Nusach Anglia or on which days. [1] [4]

Medieval England likely shared in the Ashkenazic piyut culture. The most we can securely name from the sources is a French/Western-Ashkenaz contrast on specific piyutim, not a distinct English list. [1] [4]

Still open

  • No supplied source gives a direct medieval English manuscript or printed witness for Nusach Anglia piyutim by name, occasion, or order.
  • No supplied source identifies a reconstructable English-only piyut corpus distinct from related French/Ashkenazi usages.
  • No supplied source dates the English performance of specific piyutim or proves which French/Ashkenazi selections were actually used in England.
  • Most conclusions about medieval England are only indirect inferences; none of the supplied sources directly attests a Nusach Anglia piyut list or schedule.

[1] What is the earliest Ashkenaz siddur and where might I find a copy?

miyodeya · Mike

Machzor Vitry by Rabbi Simcha ben Shmuel of Vitry was written around the year 1100. It is not only a high-holiday machzor, but also contains the services for weekdays, Shabbat, and festivals, and customs and laws following the rulings of Rashi. Rav Simcha was a talmid of Rashi, and the machzor reflects the customs of Jews in early medieval France. This is believed to be the basis for Nusach Ashkenaz. A two-volume printed edition of it can be purchased here (it says three volumes, but I think it is really two). An online edition can be found here . Be warned though that some manuscripts and printed editions of this book have extraneous material added in and do not mark those as later additions not by Rav Simcha.

In Ashkenaz today [the situation was different in different communities in the past]: Me'ora - Before or chodosh. Ahava - different minhogim, either before v'keiravtanu or before l'hodos lecho. Maarivim Short piyut before end of first berocho, Same with second; long piyut before בגילה ברינה בשמחה רבה ואמרו כלם, short piyut before זה צור ישענו פצו פה ואמרו [insted of זה א-לי ענו ואמרו], short piyut before end of that berocho, and short or long piyut before the end of השכיבנו.

Question

R' Kook's sources in Tannaic/Amoraic literature that nations don't have bechirah

R' Kook https://he.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D7%90%D7%91%D7%A8%D7%94%D7%9D_%D7%99%D7%A6%D7%97%D7%A7_%D7%94%D7%9B%D7%94%D7%9F_%D7%A7%D7%95%D7%A7 writes in many places about how while, on a personal level, there exists bechirah, on a national level no such bechirah exists. Some examples:

Ein Ayah, Berachot 9:259:

יש הבדל בין חק הכללי לחק הפרטי, ודאי הוא שהבחירה בחק הכלל אינה חפשית

There is a difference between the rules of the general populace and the rules of the individual: It certainly is that choice, in the rules of the general populace, is not free.

Mishpat Kohen p. 328:

ומה שכתב כבוד תורתו שהכל בידי שמים חוץ מיראת שמים נאמר על הפרט ועל הכלל בשוה ... הנה ב"עקידה" בפרשת נצבים מאריך הוא לבאר תוכן הברית שכרת הקב"ה עם ישראל בערבות מואב, שבוא בנוי על יסוד סילוק הבחירה מהכלל כולו, ולדבריו לא נאמר הכל בידי שמים חוץ מיראת שמים כי אם דווקא על הפרט, אבל כלל ישראל כשם שיש כריתות ברית על קיומו, כן יש כריתות ברית שלא יעזוב, בכללו, ברית ה'. ולא ראיתי מחכמי ישראל הגדולים, מי שחולק על זה.

And what his Honor said that all is in the hands of Heaven except fear of Heaven is said both on the individual and the general populace equally ... Indeed in "Akeidah" in parashat Nitzavim he elaborates extensively on the concept of the covenant that HKBH established with the Nation of Israel in the Plains of Moav, which was built on the foundation of disconnecting Choice from the entire general populace. And according to him, all is in the hands of Heaven is only said about the inidividual, but Klal Yisrael - the same that there is a covenant established with them upon their creation, there is also an oath that it will not abandon, as a whole, the covenant with Hashem. And I have not seen any of the great Sages of Israel who have disagreed with this.

Midbar Shur, Drush 34:

אין הבחירה חפשית כי אם בנוגע לפרטים של ישראל, אבל בנוגע לכלל ישראל כרת השי"ת כבר ברית עמנו, היינו שאי אפשר כלל שיהיה ח"ו כלל ישראל נבדל ונפרד מקדושת שמו הגדול ב"ה.

There's no free choice except with regard to individuals within Israel, but with regard to the entirety of Israel Hashem established a covenant with us, such that it is impossible that ch"v the entirety of Israel will be separated and removed from the holiness of His great Name.

And R' Tzvi Yehuda Kook https://he.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D7%A6%D7%91%D7%99_%D7%99%D7%94%D7%95%D7%93%D7%94_%D7%94%D7%9B%D7%94%D7%9F_%D7%A7%D7%95%D7%A7 makes it clear in his Sichot on Orot https://www.sefer.org.il/items/5799194-%D7%A9%D7%99%D7%97%D7%95%D7%AA-%D7%94%D7%A8%D7%91-%D7%A6%D7%91%D7%99-%D7%99%D7%94%D7%95%D7%93%D7%94-%D7%A2%D7%9C-%D7%A1%D7%A4%D7%A8-%D7%90%D7%95%D7%A8%D7%95%D7%AA-%D7%9C%D7%9E%D7%A8%D7%9F-%D7%94%D7%A8%D7%91-%D7%90%D7%91%D7%A8%D7%94%D7%9D-%D7%99%D7%A6%D7%97%D7%A7-%D7%94%D7%9B%D7%94%D7%9F-%D7%A7%D7%95%D7%A7-%D7%A1%D7%98-%D7%99%D7%92-%D7%9B%D7%A8%D7%9B%D7%99%D7%9D-%D7%94%D7%95%D7%A6%D7%90%D7%94-%D7%9C%D7%90%D7%95%D7%A8-%D7%A1%D7%A4%D7%A8%D7%99%D7%99%D7%AA-%D7%97%D7%95%D7%94 (Yisrael uTechiyato 3:2:4) that this is talking about all the nations of the world, not just the Jewish people. I haven't encountered this concept elsewhere outside of R' Kook (and the sefer he's referencing in the second source I quoted, which I'm not familiar with), but it is a very powerful concept. Is this idea reflected anywhere in Tannaic/Amoraic literature anywhere? Where does R' Kook get this from?

Answer

A whole nation needs special historical, moral, and communal preparation in order to sustain a refined spiritual life. That this preparation was completed only in Israel. [4]

Decrees can be altered through prayer, charity, repentance, name-change, action, place-change, and fasting. This shows strong providential shaping of collective history. [6]

The safest conclusion is that R. Kook is building from themes of covenant, providence, and collective destiny in Chazal-style language. The extension from Israel to all nations is not documented here as an explicit tannaitic or amoraic statement. [4]

They support the narrower claim that Israel’s collective is uniquely bound by covenant and that collective outcomes are not purely the sum of individual choices. Collective outcomes are not purely the sum of individual choices. [4] [6]

The supplied material supports R. Kook’s distinction between individual and collective, and between Israel and other peoples in terms of providence and covenant. But not a direct amoraic/tannaitic source for the proposition that nations as nations lack bechirah. [4]

[1] Why did the nations reject the Torah if they were obligated regardless as bnei noach?

miyodeya

Answer 1 (MichoelR): I remember hearing from Rav Yaakov Weinberg z"l, something like: The Sheva Mitzvos for Bnei Noach are not a ברית, a covenant. They don't define a relationship. They are just a set of rules posted on the wall of the hotel: If you stay here you must do the following... The Torah is very different. They were being offered the opportunity to be part of the ברית with Hashem, with Israel. They would have had a relationship. I don't think they were offered the same relationship as Israel - my clear impression from the Sifri on V'zos Habracha is that they were bound to be in a less central position (see the part about the donkey and the dog). The Midrash sounds like they would not have had 613 mitzvos; from the Midrash, more than just 7 mitzvos, but less than what Israel was given. I don't know what it would have looked like exactly. Maybe each nation would have emphasized different things? But they were offered a real covenant, and the world would be a better place if they had joined. We would not have to wait until Moshiach for all the nations to serve Hashem. So I think the answer to your question is that they never really had to accept it before. Now it was real, and they weren't willing to do that. Answer 2 (Schmerel): There are many answers given to this question. Among them are: The scope of mitzvos is different: For an aku"m, murder means unlawfully killing another person. Yet, for a Jew, murder goes far beyond that. Someone who embarrasses his friend publicly, malbin pnei chaveiro b'rabim, is guilty according to Chazal of the dust of shefichus damim, murder. The same is true of arayos, theft, and many other mitzvos. You don't need to mug someone to be guilty of gezel. If you make noise that disturbs your neighbor's sleep, it's gezel sheinah. This is what the nations of the world were rejecting. Torah is a lifestlye, not just a set of laws. What Eisav and Yishmael and the other nations were telling G-d is that their culture is one of bloodshed, theft, arayos, etc. The ben Noach laws require them to curb their natural instinct, but Torah isn't only about that. The Rambam writes in Shmoneh Perakim that the goal of Torah is to transform a person into someone who does not desire bloodshed, or theft, or other crimes -- not simply to avoid committing them. The nations could not envision changing in that way It's true that the Bnei Noach were already commanded in those laws. But here they were being asked: "Do you WANT to accept these laws?" Their response was in the negative.

[2] The Torah offered to other nations

miyodeya

Answer 1 (Menachem): There are many that pose a similar question: If the Torah was to remain in the hands of the angels, as was their demand (תנה הודך על השמים) before being bested in debate by Moshe Rabeinu, what sort of Torah were they asking for? Did they not see the obvious questions of "Were you in Egypt? Do you do work? Do you have parents? Do you have a yetzer hora ?" One answer given in seforim (source ...) is that the exact wording of the Torah had not yet been finalized. The letters were, but not the exact tzerufim . (Similar to one explanation of Moshe writing the last 8 verses בדמע - mixed up, not "in tears".) As such, the Torah as given to the angels could have been formalized differently. I imagine a similar answer could be given for other nations. A simpler answer is that the the Torah would have been offered to the Jews regardless. However, the nations were also offered a chance to follow the commandments and be rewarded for their efforts. Answer 2 (user1879026): As I understand, the source of this is Midrash Sifri, Devarim 343. It says there that aside from Esau, Moab, Ammon, and Ishmael, all other nations were approached. Now the points you raise within your question do logically point to this occurrence being unlikely. Hashem knows the Torah is not fit for any other nation. In fact, the final point of the Shema goes against this notion. "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, to be your God: I am the LORD your God" - Bamidbar 15:41 He brought us out of Egypt and that is when we became his people. No other nation has that relationship with the Kadosh Baruch-Hu. Now there's a significant amount of Rishonim and Geonim that warn us against taking all words of our Sages at face value. That they are to be examined, especially when they are not corroborated in the Tanakh and cannot be made sense of logically. Such is the case here, where the Sages in this Midrash are trying to convey a message rather than a history. Rambam in his introduction to Mishnah Tractate Sanhedrin, chapter 10 (Perek Helek) writes quite energetically against and is quite upset with literal interpretations of such works . I can provide more sources if needed. Answer 3 (Mordechai): Although the Midrash ( Sifri Dvarim 343 , Mchilta 20:2 , Tanchuma Vzot Habracha 4 ) brings verses from the Torah, it specifies afterwards that they did not even keep the 7 Noahide laws. This implies that they didn't keep the laws given to mankind and therefore they were not offered to become the chosen people and receive the whole Torah. Note that the parallel Gemara ( Bava Kama 38a ) does not mention the whole going around story and just states that the other nations did not fulfill the 7 Noahide laws.

[3] Hashgacha question - why does Hashem influence the nations to chastise us, in order for us to do Teshuva?

miyodeya · Rabbi Kaii

Hashem's entire focus is on Israel, not the nations. This is for everyone's good. When it was clear that the nations were not going to do Teshuva, Hashem, in His mercy, stopped focussing on them, so that He wouldn't find cause to destroy the world (Chesed LeAvraham Maayan 1), and chose Israel to continue His plan and purpose. He saw that the nation of Israel were worth the investment, and through Israel, the rest of the nations could eventually be redeemed (Isaiah 52-53). Secondly, if, when you say "why didn't He influence the Jews to repent", you mean that Hashem should in some way force them to repent (say, through miracle or some other free-will canceling means), then that defeats the purpose of creation. The world must do teshuva freely . If you just mean, to create pressure, or some other indirect, free-will-preserving influence, then He does indeed do that as well. For example: He sends us Prophets to come and call for us to do teshuva, such as Yirmiyahu, Amos, Hoshea. He causes suffering that are meant to be wake-up calls (Berachot 5a): If a person sees that suffering is coming upon them, they should examine their deeds. Hashem has many ways, and we know that He personally runs, down to the finest of detail, the lives of Israel (see Derech Hashem 2), and He is always devising means to save (Shmuel 2:14:14, see Arizal's comments on that in Shaar Hagilgulim). Why does He sometimes send pagan nations to be the "teshuva influencers"? This is generally part of the curses given in Ki Tavo, about how when we are not keeping the mitzvot, the nations will rule over us (see the berachot given to Esav and Yaacov). Some Kabbalistic explanations are also relevant. For example, HaRav Mendel Kessin Shlita explains that now that Hashem's focus is not on the nations, they have no connection to Him, and don't get their shefa directly from Him, but instead get it from Esav's angel. The angel also doesn't get shefa from Hashem directly, but except through the sins of Israel, so effectively, we empower the nations against us when we sin! In what manner does He influence the nations? See this passage from Chagiga 13b for an example: The verse states: “And I looked and, behold, a stormy wind came out of the north, a great cloud, with a fire flashing up, so that a brightness was round about it; and out of its midst was like the color of electrum, out of the midst of the fire” (Ezekiel 1:4). The Gemara poses a question: Where did that wind go? Rav Yehuda said that Rav said: It went to conquer the entire world under the wicked Nebuchadnezzar. And why was all of this necessary? Why was it necessary that the entire world be subjected to his dominion? So that the nations of the world would not say: The Holy One, Blessed be He, delivered His children into the hands of a lowly nation. Since it was already decreed that the kingdom of Israel would fall into the hands of Nebuchadnezzar, God made him into a great conqueror, so that Israel would not be ashamed of being defeated by him. The Holy One, Blessed be He, said with regard to this: Who caused Me to be an attendant to worshippers of molten images, forcing Me to wage their wars? It was the sins of Israel that led Me to do so. [Steinsalz translation and interlinear commentary]

[5] Isaiah 53 How does Israel's suffering atone for the sins of the nations?

miyodeya · rosends

I don't have a thorough answer about the mechanism of substitutive atonement -- in general, in Judaism, a person dies for his own sin. In those instances where it LOOKS like one is dying for another, the rabbis explain that the death of a particular person is designed to inspire others to repent and avoid their own punishment. The death brings about atonement by driving a change in the other person or people.

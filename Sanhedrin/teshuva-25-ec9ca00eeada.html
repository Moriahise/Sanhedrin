Answer

The terms Memallel (ממלל), Medaber (מדבר), and M'siach (מסיח), all relating to speech, have distinct nuances in Hebrew rooted in the nature and quality of verbal expression, particularly in a theological and philosophical context. Memallel literally means “one who utters words” or “one who articulates,” highlighting the mere act of vocalizing words. Medaber, typically translated as “speaker,” implies speaking that is intelligible, heard, and accepted—speech that carries meaning and is received by listeners. M'siach refers to engaging in conversation, a back-and-forth verbal exchange, as casual or interactive speech. This differentiation aligns with the understanding that not all vocalizations are necessarily “dibbur” (speech in its fullest sense) unless heard and accepted, and it suggests a gradation from simply uttering sounds to meaningful speech to interactive dialogue (source 1). [1]

Regarding the symbolic use of Memallel for the fourth kingdom in the four kingdoms paradigm, it is taught that humans, the fourth kingdom, are uniquely identified as "medaber" (speakers) because speech is the defining attribute distinguishing humans from other kingdoms (mineral, plant, and animal). However, Memallel is chosen rather than Medaber as the designation, emphasizing the human potential and power to produce speech — the capacity to create meaningful utterances that reveal unique individual spiritual light. Memallel connotes the raw potential to articulate words, even if not always engaged in dialogic or responsive speech (Medaber) or casual chatting (M'siach). This concept relates to the Kabbalistic and Chassidic idea that speech is rooted in the soul's essence, uniquely human, and is a channel for expressing divine intellect and individuality (source 1). [1]

Speech that is not heard, accepted, or creates impact is not considered fully Dibbur. According to the text you referred to, "Speech (Dibbur) that doesn't get heard or accepted can't be called 'speech' at all." This supports the notion that Memallel as uttering words is the initial stage. Medaber as hearing and accepting the speech presumes further degree of connection and recognition. M'siach, as conversation, is an even more interactive and recognized form of communication (source 1). [1]

The question whether porpoises are Memallel or Chai (living) is complex. Chai generally refers to the animal kingdom that moves and exhibits life. Memallel, being speech-related, classifies beings capable of speech. Since porpoises communicate through clicks and sounds but not human speech, they would be considered Chai (animals) but not Memallel. The classification of animals as Chai, and humans as Medaber or Memallel, aligns with traditional distinctions where speech sets humans apart (source 1). [1]

Regarding whether Dibbur includes written speech, the sources recognize Dibbur can refer to written communication as well as spoken. Therefore, a person illiterate but speaking a language is Memallel, as one utters spoken words. Medaber is more inclusive in encompassing not just spoken but also written and meaningful communication that is received and understood. Since speech historically precedes writing, Memallel as uttering words is the foundational stage, and Medaber encompasses both spoken and written accepted communication (source 1). [1]

The distinction between Mila (מילה), Sicha (שיחה), Dibbur (דיבור), and Memallel relates to language granularity: Mila refers simply to 'words', the fundamental units of language; Sicha denotes casual conversation or talking; Dibbur, a fuller form of speech, implies speaking with intent and reception; Memallel is the act of uttering words, the vocalization itself. Thus, Mila is about words, Sicha about informal speech, Dibbur about meaningful communication that's heard and accepted, and Memallel about producing spoken words (source 1). [1]

The Four Kingdoms model relates the progression of creation from mineral (דומם), plant (צומח), animal (חי), to human (מדבר). Humans, the fourth kingdom, are uniquely termed Medaber (speaking), as speech is their defining attribute. The label Memallel is closely related, emphasizing the ability or potential to utter or articulate words, which is a capability unique to humans in creation. This provides the theological foundation why Memallel is the archetype for the fourth kingdom (source 1). [1]

The Hebrew root ד-ב-ר underlies words associated with speech and wilderness (מדבר - midbar), indicating a conceptual link: ‘midbar,’ wilderness, is a place devoid of speech (Medaber), highlighting that speech presence defines human civilization. This enriches the significance of medaber and memallel as speech-related terms expressing human uniqueness, not mere utterance by animals or other creatures (source 2). [2]

In halachic context, speech is defined as articulation with intent and recognition. Sefarim discussing Shevuot (oaths) clarify that mere writing or texting may not fulfill speech requirements that involve pronunciation with lips. However, Dibbur can include written forms insofar as they transmit meaningful communication. Since Memallel involves uttering words (spoken), and Medaber involves communication that is heard and accepted, the written word counts more properly as Medaber (source 4). [4]

In summary, Memallel is the stage of vocalizing words; Medaber is communicating speech that is heard, accepted, or written and understood; and M'siach is the interactive element of conversation. Chai refers to living creatures generally, including animals like porpoises, which do not qualify as Memallel or Medaber as they lack human speech capacity. Memallel as the label for the fourth kingdom emphasizes the unique faculty of human speech potential as rooted in the soul and divine image (source 1). [1]

[1] From Golems to AI

chabad · Yehuda Shurpin

As the wise King Solomon put it, “There is nothing new under the sun.”1 The concept of man-made men (golems), and the question of their humanness (or lack thereof) has been discussed since Talmudic times. (Of course, there is a huge difference between a “living” golem and a man-made robot powered by AI, but this would seem to be the best place to start our discussion). The Talmud relates that Rava once created a “man” through the mystical codes within the Sefer Yetzirah (“Book of Formation”). He then sent this man to Rabbi Zeira, who spoke to it, but the man was incapable of speech and did not reply. Rabbi Zeira then said to it, “You are a creation of one of my colleagues; return to your dust!”2 The Approach of the Chacham Tzvi Was this “man” created by Rava considered human? Was Rabbi Zeira liable for murder? The biblical term for “human” is often “[one who was] born from a woman,”3 implying that the definition of a human is one who was born from one. Additionally, Scripture describes murder as “spilling the blood of a human with[in] a human.”4 Rabbi Tzvi Hirsch Ashkenazi (known as the Chacham Tzvi, 1656–1718), whose own grandfather Rabbi Eliyahu of Chelm reportedly created a “man,”explains that only one who kills a person that was formed within another human being is liable for murder. Thus, killing a being created from another source does not constitute murder.5 The question of murder aside, would this “man” be Jewish and be counted for a minyan? The Chacham Tzvi cites the rabbinic teaching that “the works of the righteous are their offspring.” Thus, one might consider a creation of a (righteous) Jew to be Jewish. However, he notes that since Rabbi Zeira did not hesitate to destroy Rava’s creation, it is evident that it was not qualified to count for a minyan. For had there been even a minimal use for this “man,” to destroy it would have been wasteful.6 Rabbi Tzvi Hirsh Shapiro of Munkacs (1850–1913), in his work Darkei Teshuvah, points out that since the Chacham Tzvi (and others7) needed to cite a specific source in Scripture to prove that killing a humanoid did not constitute murder, apparently he held that it may very well have some element of humanness, independent of the question of murder. He therefore entertains the question of whether the shechitah (“kosher slaughter”) performed by such a creation would be valid.8 The notion that a golem may have an element of humanness left some, including the Chacham Tzvi’s two sons, a bit puzzled. It Has No Soul Both sons, Rabbi Avraham Meshulam Zalman, in his work Divrei Rabeinu Meshulam,9 and Rabbi Yaakov Emden in Sheilot Yaavetz,10 quote Kabbalists,11 who explain that only G‑d has the power to draw a human soul down from heaven. At best, a person using the power of the Sefer Yetzirah can only animate something on par with an animal. It is for this reason that if one “killed” such a creature (as in the story with Rava), they say, it is not considered murder. From Golems to Robots It should be noted that unlike a robot, a golem has some sort of a spiritual spark animating it. It is brought to life through a righteous individual using the secrets of creation hidden within the Sefer Yetzirah. This is clearly not the case for a man-made robot powered by algorithms. Thus, our robots, powered by computers, are seemingly even less “human” than a golem. Nevertheless, putting that aside for argument’s sake, there is one aspect where a robot may have an advantage over a golem. Artificial Intelligence and Speech Everything in the world can be divided into four “kingdoms”: mineral, vegetable, animal, and human. The word for “human” is medaber, which means “speaker.”12 This implies that the ability to speak is integral to who we are as humans. Thus, unlike the Chacham Tzvi, many explain that the reason we can’t count a golem for a minyan is that it lacks the faculty of speech. At first blush, this would imply that if the creature could just talk (as today’s robots certainly can), it would be considered human. However, as many point out, the key defining characteristic of humanity cannot be speech alone, for there are people who cannot speak—and parrots that can.13 Therefore, they explain that when we refer to humanity as medaber, the actual intent is intelligence.14 Based on this, there are some who have made the surprising claim that if one would somehow make an intelligent and speaking golem (a feat many mystics say is theoretically possible15) it perhaps could be counted for a minyan.16 If this is true for a golem, then perhaps it would be true for a robot powered by AI! However, many point out that when the rabbis say “intelligence,” they aren’t merely referring to the collection of data and facts, or even the ability to analyze and problem-solve, but to what some would call “moral intelligence,” or as others put it, “free will.”17 It All Comes Together: Speech, Intellect and Soul The chassidic masters question why humanity is defined as medaber (“speaker”) and not maskil (“understander”).18 After all, as we have pointed out, there are people who are unable to talk and animals that can! Rabbi Sholom Dovber of Lubavitch explains that the use of the term medaber is indeed precise; however, it doesn’t just refer to the ability to talk, but rather to the koach hadibur, the potential or power to talk.19 Human speech is different than any other similar type of communication, for it is not merely an external “revelation”; rather, it reveals what is "hidden" inside the person. Certainly, one can parrot words and sounds, but medaber refers to the power that gives shape, letters and words to one’s thoughts, which are then spoken with one's mouth. The chassidic masters explain that the faculty of speech is in fact rooted in the essence of one’s soul, and it is therefore much higher and deeper than intellect itself. Thus, although two people may have the same exact thought, they express it in their own unique, individual way. Thus, even when we characterize humans as medaber, “those who talk,” we are essentially also characterizing them as having unique, G‑d-given souls. And that is something that AI cannot replicate. [2] What does midbar mean?

miyodeya

Answer 1 (Yaacov Deane): The noun Midbar is best translated as wilderness , not necessarily desert . The distinction being that a wilderness is a place devoid of human habitation and settlements. The term desert , although coincidentally usually not conducive to human settlement, is only about a lack of precipitation, rainfall or snowfall. And to emphasize, it doesn’t necessarily imply a lack of availability of water. A desert can be filled with wellsprings, underground aquifers or like the continent of Antarctica can be covered with ice and snow because whatever precipitation has ever fallen there over the millennia has not evaporated because of the extreme and continuous cold. So returning to the subject of wilderness being devoid of human settlement, the Torah, in its categorization of different types of creations, has four general categories of: inanimate objects דומם (things which are silent and do not have their own power of locomotion), plants צומח (things which sprout, usually, but not limited to sprouting from the ground), animals חיות (meaning things which do not sprout and which possess the power of self-locomotion. They may also be able to make noises and even possess some form of communication other than speech.) and humanoids מדבר (beings which have the power of speech, either oral or written or both). And this underlines the association of wilderness מדבר (pronounced Midbar) and humanoid מדבר (pronounced M’Daber, meaning speak or speech) settlement. That a wilderness can be profusely filled with all kinds of life, but is devoid of speech. Answer 2 (Shmuel): Rabbi Yonathan Sacks z"l explained once that the meaning behind the word "midbar" is to mean (COVENANT & CONVERSATION The Wilderness and the Word, Bamidbar 5768) : The Hebrew word midbar, wilderness, has the same root as the word dabar/davar, meaning “word” or “thing.” It has the same letters as medabber, “speaking.” It is in the wilderness that the Israelites hear revelation, the word or speaking of G-d There's a famous Midrash (see for example here ) that explains why the Torah has been given in the midbar. Why was the Torah not given in the Holy Land, Eretz Yisrael? The answer lies, tells the Midrash, in the fact that a midbar has no ownership on itself. A midbar belongs to no one. So too, everyone has an equal claim to the Torah (see also Midrash Tanchuma, Chukas 21:1) Elsewhere , Rabbi Sacks explains the deeper meaning behind the reason why the Torah was given in the midbar: The giving of the Torah in the wilderness is an essential feature of Jewish history. Had the Israelites received the Torah in the land, it would be indissolubly associated with the land. Exile would mean the end of the covenant. It would make no more sense to keep Torah while in exile than to obey the laws of Russia while living in Spain. What made the G-d of Israel different was the fact that He was sovereign of the universe, not a local deity. That is why the Jewish people survived dispersion. Only the G-d of everywhere can be found and worshipped anywhere. I would strongly recommend this article, to further grasp the essence of the word "midbar" and the relationship between the giving of the Torah and us being in exile. https://www.rabbisacks.org/covenant-conversation/bamidbar/wilderness-and-revelation/

Answer 1 (ray): in chovos halevavos shaar bechina "It is proper that you should know that the whole world (even inanimate things - Tov Halevanon) is synthesized of the physical and the spiritual, so intimately mixed and fused, that each of them sustains the other, like body and soul in living creatures." pas lechem commentary there on the words "like body and soul (nefesh) in living creatures": the soul prolongs and sustains the body. When the soul leaves the body, the body immediately inanimates and decomposes. Likewise, the body sustains the animal soul in animals, and when the animal soul leaves the animal's body, it (the animal soul) ceases and nullifies. Likewise, for the nefesh tzomachat (plant soul) in plants. For the nefesh medaberet (human soul) we can say the same regarding its existing in this physical world, because immediately after the human soul leaves the body, it returns to its place in the higher worlds [as written (Eccl. 12:7) "Then shall the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto G-d who gave it."]. hence the animal soul is lower level than human soul and cannot exist independently of the animal body unlike human higher soul which is independent of the human body and can continue to exist without it. As we learn from Rashi on Bereshis 1:1 and Bereshis Rabba 1:4 , that the world was created bereishis , in the merit of reishis , i.e. Israel among the nations. Just as the first produce is sacred so is Israel, G-d's sacred nation. Yet, while we are the very purpose for which the world was created, in the next breath the Torah foreshadows the trials and tribulations we must endure throughout our history. The Divine Plan for human destiny is thereby framed at the outset by the verses Bereshis 1:1-2 , from day One to the End of Days. The commentary Yefeh To'ar (1), which views the verse Bereshis 1:2 as a remez to the 4 kingdoms explains the idea of Creation with the following excerpt: The final point of Creation is the time of moshiyach , when the world gets settles in peace, and be filled with knowledge, and everyone will speak in the name of Hashem. Hashem did not refrain from creating the world as He conceived the idea of 4 kingdoms, despite the fact that multitude of their sinful acts and variants of faith were rebellion to His Honor. Hashem knows the final end, and because of it everything perfomed for our forefathers from the time of their redemption from Egypt and building the Temple - is an afront to His final goal, and so in the end it was all destroyed as if He acted for naught. And His answer was that everything was due to the nature of the matter. The completeness of the entire human race was withheld from others for a long time, because the nature of their creation did not accept it from its beginning. For this it took a long time with many tests and trials and rebukes from several prophets and many troubles in bitter and difficult revelations. After that the redeemed (Jewish) people came out clean as pure and refined silver and returned to Hashem with all their heart and with all their soul. Thus, they knew faithfully that there was no truth and rejected all the kinds of deceptions, which only served to turn away people from from Hashem. Yes, they were ashamed and deceived by their deeds and recognized that there was no other remedy but to seek refuge under His wings. Thus, at the end they will come to Hashem in a covenant of peace (as granted to Pinchas) which will never be broken. The idea of four kingdoms can schematically be represented by the 4 letters of the dreidel (theme 58 in (2) and (3), also Bnei Yisaschar) or in the word גֹשְנָה - the place of the first exile in Mitzrayim, where נ - nefesh , ג - guf , ש - sekhel , and ה - hakol or neshamah . Every exile among the nations sought to target the Jewish nation in different ways. Galus Bavel destroyed the First Temple, rendering it impossible for the Jews to perfect their souls ( nefesh ) through sacrifices (see Vayikra 2:1). The Persian/Median exile threatened our physical existence ( guf ) because of Haman's plot. The Greeks sought to supplant our knowledge base sekhel with their own, and when they could not win by intellectual superiority, they attempted to suppress the study of Torah. Finally Edom/Rome attacked us in every way (hakol). The Gematria of the word משיח is 358 = ג+נ+ה+ש. Thus the the Messianic king will be able to overcome hakol . Alternatively, the final teshuvah and overcoming of each of the kingdoms would allow the neshamah of the person enlighten his/her guf , nefesh and sekhel . The midrash connects each of galuyos using similar words. The verse Yirmiyahu 4:23 describes the prophetic vision of the prophet Yirmiyahu about what was going to happen to Eretz Yisrael as a result of the conquest by Nevukhadnetzar king of Bavel. The army of Bavel would exile the people, destroy the Temple, and make the land empty and desolate (תֹ֖הוּ וָבֹ֑הוּ), same words as in Bereshis 1:2 . In the language of Kabbala, the exile targeted the nefesh of the Jewish people, as being uprooted from their land and subject to an environment of a different country. The verse Esther 6:14 speaks how providentially the Persian king's eunuchs came in the middle of the argument between Haman and Zeresh and hurriedly (וַיַּבְהִ֙לוּ֙, i.e. astonished him) rushed Haman to Esther's banquet. The word וַיַּבְהִ֙לוּ֙ has root בהל - shock, astonish, which is similar to the word ּבֹּהו - astonishingly empty as pointed by Matanos Kehuna. To explain the connection pointed by Matnos Kehuna, one can used the verse Tehilim 30:8 : for You, O LORD, when You were pleased, made [me] firm as a mighty mountain. When You hid (הִסְתַּ֥רְתָּ) Your face, I was terrified (נִבְהָֽל). I.e. just like the created אֶרֶץ was called בֹּהוּ in Bereshis 1:2 because it was void of רוּחַ אֱלֹהִ֔ים, so is a person who is נִבְהָֽל because s/he experiences the הסתר פנים של השם. In other words, upto the moment of the verse Esther 6:14 Haman felt empowered while the Jews were נִבְהָֽלִים - astonishingly frightened, as if Hashem was letting Haman do with his conspiracy to destroy Jewish people. At the moment when Haman was called to the banquet, right after Zeresh told him that he had no chance against Mordekhai, the roles changed: Haman realized that the possibility to make his evil plans was an illusion and he was going to face justice. Haman himself became נִבְהָֽל and the Jewish people realized that they have a shot to win the battle! To understand the language of Kabbala, Rashi explains, that Harvonah overheard the conversation between Haman and Zeresh, where Haman expressed a wish to convince the king for the second time to hang Mordechai, ... and from here Harvonah knew why Haman ordered building the gallows. As we understand, the gallows is made to destroy the body. The quote about the Greek Kingdom seems cryptic. For direct answer, one can use Yerushalmi Chagiga 2:2:4 stating “Hillel and Menaḥem did not disagree. Menaḥem left and Shammai entered.” Where did he leave to? Some are saying, from behavior he left to behavior. But some are saying, he left against his face, he and eighty pairs of students of the Sages, wearing golden tirqe, whose faces became black like the rim of a clay pot , when they were told (by the Greeks), write on a bull’s horn that you have no part in the God of Israel . In fact every Jew, who owned an ox was commanded to do so and publicize the heresy. Yefeh To'ar states that the decree of the Greeks hints to the making of the Golden Calf which distanced us from Hashem. The reason why this answer works, comes from another midrash, Bamidbar Rabba 2:10 , which talks about different directions symbolized by the camps in the desert. In particular, North, from there darkness emerges to the world, and corresponding to it, the tribe of Dan. Why? It is because it brought darkness to the world with idol worship. Yerovam crafted two golden calves, and idol worship is darkness, as it is stated: “ Their actions are in the dark (בְמַחְשָׁךְ֙) ” ( Isaiah 29:15 ). Yerovam circulated throughout Israel and it was only the tribe of Dan that accepted it from him, as it is stated: “The king consulted, and he crafted two golden calves…and he placed one in Dan” (I Kings 12:28–29). That is why the Holy One blessed be He commanded that Dan would encamp in the north. That is what is written: “The banner of the camp of Dan is to the north…” (Numbers 2:25). With it, the tribe of Asher, which illuminates the darkness, as it is stated: “And of Asher he said: Blessed…and he dips his foot in oil” (Deuteronomy 33:24). Likewise it says: “And those who encamp with it, the tribe of Asher” (Numbers 2:27). With it, Naphtali, who is filled with blessing in order to provide sustenance, just as it is written: “Naphtali, his desire is satisfied, [and full with the blessing of the Lord]” (Deuteronomy 33:23). Likewise it says: “and the tribe of Naphtali” (Numbers 2:29). “All those counted for the camp of Dan…they shall travel last by their banners (Numbers 2:31), as anyone who engages in idol worship goes backward and not forward. That is why it is stated: “The Lord founded the earth with wisdom, established the heavens with understanding”(Proverbs 3:19). The key point is, the kingdom of Yavan (Greece) was forcing upon Jewish people all different ways of heresy and avoda zara , with decrees to reject G-d of Israel, and the verse Isaiah 29:15 links avoda zara and darkness. There are also circumstantial indications in other sources. For example, Megilas Taanis Adar בח' בטבת נכתבה התורה יונית בימי תלמי המלך והחושך בא לעולם שלשת ימים. On the 8th day of Teves the Torah was translated into Greek language in the days of king Ptolemy II and darkness (חושך) fell on the world for 3 days. (On the kabbala level, the skin of the ox is used to make the batim for tefilin and parchments for Torah, tefilin, and mezuzos - items that symbolize the foundations of our faith and connection to the Torah. The fact that the Greeks forbade learning of the Torah - was a decree against our sekhel ). Interestingly, the mishna Mikvaos 9:2 also gives the answer which fits the language of the midrash. Among the things which prevent purification in the mikveh , the mishnah mentions טִיט הַיָּוֵן - miry, slimy clay and גֵץ יְוֵנִי - road clay, dirt: What is meant by 'miry clay’? This means the clay in pits, for it is written: "He lifted me out of the miry pit, the slimy clay (מִטִּיט הַיָּוֵן)" ( Psalms 40:3 )... And "road-clay." These become like road-side pegs in these [kinds of clay] one may not immerse oneself nor immerse [other things] with them. The root of the word יָּוֵן is the same as יון - the Hebrew name for Greece. Just like dirt prevents one to achieve purity, so did the Greek wisdom when applied to the wisdom of the Torah. For example, the Greeks denied that humans had ability to receive divine prophecy and that the laws of the Torah were divinely ordained. Such a view devalues the mitzvos and the Torah. Regarding the kingdom of Edom, Yefeh To'ar gives two answers about the language מַה הַתְּהוֹם הַזֶּה אֵין לוֹ חֵקֶר - just as the depths cannot be calculated . Yefeh To'ar states that the evil kingdom of Edom was great and tenthfold mighty (i.e. its power cannot be calculated). The Romans recruited conscripts into their army people from all nations of the world ( Bereshis Rabba 42:4 ). In the army they would forget their native language and all become Romans so that there would not be distinction between people of the empire. Even with respect to religion, the Romans modified the beliefs of the conquered nations so that their faiths became variants of the single imperial cult. In this respect, the empire was similar to the deep waters, where one cannot see anything and distinguish one thing from another. As every nation has its own good or bad inclinations, the mix creates the confusion when anything of value for neshamah could easily be targeted. Alternatively, Yefeh To'ar with reference to ha-Arukh the words Bereshis Rabba 42:4 מַכְתֶּבֶת טִירוֹנְיָא מִכָּל אֻמּוֹת הָעוֹלָם as collecting taxes from all nations meaning such a large sum of money which cannot be calculated . The second answer of Yefeh Toar is based on Gemara Pesachim 54b : the Sages taught: Seven matters are concealed from people, and they are: The day of death; and the day of consolation from one’s concerns; the profundity of justice, ascertaining the truth in certain disputes; and a person also does not know what is in the heart of another; and a person does not know in what way he will earn a profit; and one does not know when the monarchy of the house of David will be restored to Israel; and when the wicked Roman monarchy will cease to exist . That is we cannot calculate the ending date of this kingdom. One can also adress the words מַה הַתְּהוֹם הַזֶּה אֵין לוֹ חֵקֶר directly with the verse Tehilim 42:8 : Where deep calls to deep (תְּהוֹם־אֶל־תְּה֣וֹם) in the roar of Your cataracts; all Your breakers and billows have swept over me. Rashi here says: Deep calls to deep One trouble calls the next one. I.e. many troubles, hard to calculate. Radak here is even more explicit: The deep . A hint at the troubles of the exile, which are likened to water, as if the troubles call out to each other: in the roar of Your cataracts . That is, my soul is humbled at the roar of your cataracts (conduits), meaning that thus the troubles continue upon us like water pouring from the roof from the conduit and making a sound, and it must be interpreted that the clouds will be called cataracts because they are conduits of rain and everything is a metaphor for the troubles: One can even include here the quoted Mishna Mikvaos 9:2 with reference to deep wells filled with muddy water preventing any purification and atonement for the sins. With absense of Beis haMikdash we do not have any prophetic insights to the end of the exile or to the end any of the difficulties that we have to live with and with any account for impurities that we may carry. Finally, regarding Moshiyach, the midrash uses Isaiah 11:2 , which speaks about time of moshiyach with completion of the man, as Malbim explains: And it rested upon him , the perfection will be in three things, a. The perfections that depend on the fact that he has thinking nefesh , with strength, chokhmah , and binah . The chokhmah will be passed by tradition from the rabbis, and binah developing distinctions in matter by means of comparisons. b. the perfection of him as a man, which includes the perfection in ability to give advice ('eitzah) and ability of might ( g'vurah , i.e. guf ). The power of advice as he will contemplate in every matter tenthfold. The power of might will come from completion of his physical development. c. The perfections that depends on the fact that he is a holy, noble soul from on high whose elevation is higher than the thinking nefesh , and the perfection of this soul depends on whether he will have the knowledge of God, that is, to know true opinions in the truth of God, His providence and His ways. Such level was achieved by the prophets who communicated these truths in prophecy. And let him have a spirit of fear of God, for knowledge without fear is nothing, and likewise with fear without knowledge one does not know whom he is to fear.

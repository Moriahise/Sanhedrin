Question

Memallel vs. M'daber vs. M'Siach

Related to this question and the comments on it, because originally by mistake I called the speaking kingdom Medaber instead of Memallel.

Are porpoises considered Memallel or Chai?

Why is Memallel chosen to be the label for the fourth kingdom? I take it part of it is that speech that is not heard or accepted as per below, is not called Dibbur, so I can see what Medaber is. Sicha is casual conversation. Mila refers to words.

https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Translation:Likutei_Etzot#DIBBUR_-_Speech

Speech (Dibbur) that doesn't get heard or accepted can't be called "speech" at all. Tikkun haDibbur, when it's heard and accepted, comes by saying words of praise of the True Tzaddikim. This is Tikkun haKlali, General Remedy, for your speech (29:1,2).
But what is Memallel versus now there's Dinbur and Mila so two things to differentiate from, and the answer coming to me, that Memallel is one who utters words. Medaber is someone whose speech (or written words?) is heard or accepted. M'Siach is someone who converses, that you can chat with. Is this correct, or no?

Also, Dibbur can refer to written speech, so one could be illiterate yet still speaks the words of the language and is therefore Memallel. I guess M'daber is therefore more inclusive, because it includes also up someone who writes but doesn't speak. Since speech came and comes before writing, I guess I've answered my own question. Unless I'm mistaken

Answer

Onkelos translates אדם as a ‘living, speaking soul,’. Rashi explains that the special feature added to האדם is ‘understanding and speech.’. Source 9 likewise defines the fourth category as beings with the power of speech, oral or written or both. [1] [2] [9]

In that light, מְמַלֵּל in Onkelos is not a separate level from מְדַבֵּר; it is simply Onkelos’s Aramaic way of describing the human as a speaking being. Source 1 uses רוח ממללא for Adam, while source 2 says the human uniquely received speech. Source 9 then treats ‘medaber’ as the standard label for the human category. [1] [9]

Your proposed distinction that מְדַבֵּר means speech that is heard or accepted is not established by the sources as a general dictionary definition. The closest source is Likutei Etzot, which says that speech that is not heard or accepted ‘cannot be called speech’ in that author’s discussion of tikkun ha-dibbur. But that is a specific spiritual teaching about repair of speech, not a general lexical rule for Hebrew. [8]

Likewise, שיחה in the supplied sources is narrower than ordinary speech: it means casual, idle, or unnecessary conversation. Yein Levanon explicitly contrasts דיבור, which includes needed household speech and teaching, with שיחה, which refers to trivial talk. Midrash Shmuel says the same. [5] [6]

The sources also show that ‘speech’ can include writing in a broader sense of communication. Source 10 develops the idea that writing is the ‘messenger of speech.’. [9] [10]

On why the fourth level is labeled ‘speaking’ at all: the sources present speech as the distinctive feature of the human being. Rashi says that unlike animals, האדם was granted speech; source 9 makes that the definition of medaber;. [2] [9]

In short: מְמַלֵּל and מְדַבֵּר are overlapping labels for the human speaking faculty in these sources; מְשִׂיחַ is casual conversation;. And the ‘heard/accepted’ idea is a particular teaching about תיקון הדיבור, not a general definition. The sources therefore support a clean distinction between human speech and animal life, but not a three-way technical taxonomy of memallel/medaber/msiach as separate creation-level categories. [1] [8] [2]

Still open

  • No supplied source explicitly discusses porpoises or any non-human animal in this terminology, so their classification can only be inferred indirectly.
  • No supplied source gives a formal lexical distinction between מְמַלֵּל and מְדַבֵּר beyond Onkelos’s translation and later explanatory comments.
  • The porpoise classification is only an inference from the human/animal distinction; no supplied source mentions porpoises directly.
  • The heard/accepted distinction for דיבור is supported only as a specific teaching in Likutei Etzot, not as a general lexical definition.

[9] What does midbar mean?

miyodeya

Answer 1 (Yaacov Deane): The noun Midbar is best translated as wilderness , not necessarily desert . The distinction being that a wilderness is a place devoid of human habitation and settlements. The term desert , although coincidentally usually not conducive to human settlement, is only about a lack of precipitation, rainfall or snowfall. And to emphasize, it doesn’t necessarily imply a lack of availability of water. A desert can be filled with wellsprings, underground aquifers or like the continent of Antarctica can be covered with ice and snow because whatever precipitation has ever fallen there over the millennia has not evaporated because of the extreme and continuous cold. So returning to the subject of wilderness being devoid of human settlement, the Torah, in its categorization of different types of creations, has four general categories of: inanimate objects דומם (things which are silent and do not have their own power of locomotion), plants צומח (things which sprout, usually, but not limited to sprouting from the ground), animals חיות (meaning things which do not sprout and which possess the power of self-locomotion. They may also be able to make noises and even possess some form of communication other than speech.) and humanoids מדבר (beings which have the power of speech, either oral or written or both). And this underlines the association of wilderness מדבר (pronounced Midbar) and humanoid מדבר (pronounced M’Daber, meaning speak or speech) settlement. That a wilderness can be profusely filled with all kinds of life, but is devoid of speech. Answer 2 (Shmuel): Rabbi Yonathan Sacks z"l explained once that the meaning behind the word "midbar" is to mean (COVENANT & CONVERSATION The Wilderness and the Word, Bamidbar 5768) : The Hebrew word midbar, wilderness, has the same root as the word dabar/davar, meaning “word” or “thing.” It has the same letters as medabber, “speaking.” It is in the wilderness that the Israelites hear revelation, the word or speaking of G-d There's a famous Midrash (see for example here ) that explains why the Torah has been given in the midbar. Why was the Torah not given in the Holy Land, Eretz Yisrael? The answer lies, tells the Midrash, in the fact that a midbar has no ownership on itself. A midbar belongs to no one. So too, everyone has an equal claim to the Torah (see also Midrash Tanchuma, Chukas 21:1) Elsewhere , Rabbi Sacks explains the deeper meaning behind the reason why the Torah was given in the midbar: The giving of the Torah in the wilderness is an essential feature of Jewish history. Had the Israelites received the Torah in the land, it would be indissolubly associated with the land. Exile would mean the end of the covenant. It would make no more sense to keep Torah while in exile than to obey the laws of Russia while living in Spain. What made the G-d of Israel different was the fact that He was sovereign of the universe, not a local deity. That is why the Jewish people survived dispersion. Only the G-d of everywhere can be found and worshipped anywhere. I would strongly recommend this article, to further grasp the essence of the word "midbar" and the relationship between the giving of the Torah and us being in exile. https://www.rabbisacks.org/covenant-conversation/bamidbar/wilderness-and-revelation/

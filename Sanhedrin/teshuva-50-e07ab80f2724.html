Question

How do different Rishonim and Acharonim count the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy (Shlosh Esrei Midot)?

The Torah lists the Shlosh Esrei Midot HaRachamim (Thirteen Attributes of Mercy) in Shemot 34:6–7. While the Talmud (Rosh Hashanah 17b) establishes that there are exactly thirteen attributes, the text itself contains more than thirteen descriptive terms, leading to a variety of opinions on exactly how the words are split and counted.

I am looking for a comprehensive breakdown or overview of how the primary Rishonim delineate the count. Specifically, how do the following authorities map out the thirteen attributes?

Rabenu Tam (and the Tosafot) The AriZal / Kabbalistic tradition The Ibn Ezra The Rambam The Meiri The Maharal Sefer Chasidim

If possible, please contrast where these lists differ (e.g., which words are split, and where the thirteen end).

Answer

The supplied sources show four main models. One that starts at the first Divine Name. One that starts at the second Divine Name. One that starts with El. One that starts only with El Rachum. They also disagree whether the ending is venakeh alone or venakeh lo yenakeh together. The Rambam does not really give a split-list at all but explains that these are descriptions of God’s actions, not divine “qualities” in the strict sense. [1] [2] [3]

The supplied summary attributes to the French Tosafists and to Rabbeinu Tam a count that begins with the first YHVH. The opening pair “YHVH, YHVH” are counted as the first two attributes. Followed by El, Rachum, Chanun, and so on. [1]

The AriZal / kabbalistic tradition in the supplied sources starts later: it counts El as the first of the thirteen. The sequence is: El, Rachum, Chanun, Erech Apayim, Rav Chesed, Emet, Notzer Chesed la’alafim, Noseh Avon, VeFesha, VeChata’ah, VeNakeh. The secondary source explicitly says this is the Arizal’s approach. [1]

He rejects counting the first Divine Name as part of the tally. Counts venakeh as its own attribute. He also counts poked avon avot as a mercy-attribute. [2]

The Rambam’s position in the supplied sources is not a “counting scheme” in the same sense. He says the thirteen middot are really descriptions of divine actions by which God is known. Not attributes in the human psychological sense. He does not give a word-by-word split in the source text provided. [3]

Rabbeinu Tam/Tosafot are presented as beginning with the first YHVH. The Arizal and Maharal are presented as beginning with El. The Rambam does not frame the issue as a technical split at all. [1] [3]

Still open

  • A primary source text for the Meiri’s counting scheme is not included, so his exact division cannot be verified from the supplied material.
  • A primary source text for Sefer Chasidim’s counting scheme is not included, so its exact division can only be reported secondhand from the supplied summary.

The second Name of Havayah — (as in Rabbeinu Gershom in Tosafos there; a Gaon brought in Ibn Ezra there, and according to the introduction of Ibn Ezra to his commentary it is Rav Saadia Gaon; and it is also found somewhat in one commentary in Sefer HaLikutim of the Arizal, Parashas Ki Tisa). “E-l” (G-d) — (as in Zohar III 131b; see Shaar HaArich Anpin chapter 11; Pri Etz Chaim, Shaar HaSelichos chapter 4; Sefer HaKavanot, discourses on Vaya’avor, from the third discourse onward; Mishnas Chassidim, tractate She’eiris HaAtzilus chapter 10 and onwards, and more. And what is explained in Derech Emes on Zohar I 2a, section 4, in the first opinion, requires further analysis. And were I not afraid, I would say it is not from Rav Chaim Vital, since it is the opposite of what is explicitly explained everywhere in the writings of the Arizal, and it has already been written that several notes from Imrei Binah were mixed in by mistake of printers in Derech Emes). “El Rachum” (Merciful G-d) — (as in Sefer Chassidim §213; Maharal of Prague in Netivos Olam, Netiv HaTeshuvah chapter 6; and what is cited there as proof from Zohar III and from the Talmud—I have not merited to understand, for on the contrary, it proves like the opinion of the Arizal). And the decision in matters of esoteric Torah — must follow the ruling of the sages of Kabbalah, particularly since they also considered the words of Tosafos, Rabbeinu Gershom, and others. And nevertheless they concluded as above. And already the Chida wrote in his commentary to the Siddur HaShelah there, as follows: “Our master (the Arizal) and Tosafos in Rosh HaShanah investigated how to count the Thirteen Attributes, because in their days the holy Zohar had not yet been revealed. And in the Zohar and in the writings of our master the Arizal their order and their secret were revealed to us.” And according to the revealed Torah one may say it depends on the textual version of the Talmud. For the version of the Rif and the Rosh is that the teaching of Rabbi Yochanan—‘a covenant is established with the Thirteen Attributes’—comes immediately after the teaching on ‘Havayah, Havayah.’ Therefore one can say that this is the beginning of the Thirteen Attributes, and this explains the connection of the Rosh and the Tosafists (whose heads were Rabbenu Tam, etc.). Similarly with the Rif and the Geonim. However, according to the version of the Talmud that has spread and been accepted by us, the teaching of ‘a covenant is established with the Thirteen Attributes’ is preceded in the Talmud with the words E-l, Rachum, etc. (and so too is the version in the Ein Yaakov). This is therefore a decisive proof that the beginning of the count is E-l, as Maharal writes there, and as also brought in Etz Yosef. (And in Dikdukei Sofrim no change of version was brought here). Now, the Rosh asks there: if not according to Rabbenu Tam’s opinion, why is the custom that when the congregation recites the Thirteen Attributes aloud, they begin from the words Havayah, Havayah? And the answer is clear according to what is written in the Arizal’s works: these two Names of Havayah are the source of all the Thirteen Attributes. And this is also understood according to Chassidus from what is written in Likkutei Torah, discourse “Vehayah bayom hahu yitaka,” and in Torah Or, Parashas Miketz, at the end of the discourse “Ki Atah Neri.” And according to this one can also explain simply the words of the Pesikta and Shocher Tov, according to the Arizal. The words of the congregation are thus: “(You) Havayah, Havayah (we request that You be) E-l Rachum, etc.” As for the further question of the Rosh, that “Notzer chesed la’alafim” (“preserving kindness for thousands”) should count as one attribute—this is already resolved in Tosafos there. And this resembles the principle: the halacha follows Rav in prohibitions, and Shmuel in civil law (see Rosh, Bava Kama 27b; see also Shulchan Aruch of the Alter Rebbe, Orach Chaim 268, Sefer Shaar HaKolel, and his Tehillato). The words “all my goodness” imply that God promised to show him the whole creation, concerning which it has been stated, “And God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good” (Gen. 1:31); when I say “to show him the whole creation,” I mean to imply that God promised to make him comprehend the nature of all things, their relation to each other, and the way they are governed by God both in reference to the universe as a whole and to each creature in particular. This knowledge is referred to when we are told of Moses,” he is firmly established in all mine house” (Num. 12:7); that is, “his knowledge of all the creatures in My universe is correct and firmly established”; for false opinions are not firmly established. Consequently the knowledge of the works of God is the knowledge of His attributes, by which He can be known. The fact that God promised Moses to give him a knowledge of His works, may be inferred from the circumstance that God taught him such attributes as refer exclusively to His works, viz., “merciful and gracious, longsuffering and abundant in goodness,” etc., (Exod. 34:6). It is therefore clear that the ways which Moses wished to know, and which God taught him, are the actions emanating from God. Our Sages call them middot (qualities), and speak of the thirteen middoth of God (Talm. B. Rosh ha-shanah, p. 17b); they used the term also in reference to man; comp. “there are four different middoth (characters) among those who go to the house of learning”; “There are four different middoth (characters) among those who give charity” (Mishnah Abot, 5:13, 14). They do not mean to say that God really possesses middot (qualities), but that He performs actions similar to such of our actions as originate in certain qualities, i.e., in certain psychical dispositions not that God has really such dispositions.
